## Abstract

SummaryThis case report provides details of the first documented case of pituitary stalk interruption syndrome (PSIS) with coexistent focal cortical dysplasia (FCD) in a young boy. The child’s initial presentation was an afebrile, generalised tonic-clonic seizure associated with postictal drowsiness. During his first episode, the physical examination revealed a short, obese child with a micropenis and left cryptorchidism. The neurological examination was normal, suggesting possible endocrine pathology. At that time, laboratory investigations showed a normal complete blood count; electrolyte levels were normal, while the thyroid function test revealed abnormal findings indicative of secondary hypothyroidism. Based on his clinical presentation, physical examination and laboratory investigations, a brain MRI scan was performed, which showed PSIS with diffuse FCD. Subsequently, anterior pituitary gland hormone levels were measured, revealing panhypopituitarism. This discovery is novel in the literature, offers potential new insights into the pathophysiology of PSIS and emphasises the importance of considering structural brain abnormalities in PSIS patients with seizures, thereby opening new avenues for future research on their neurological consequences.

## Body

Background Pituitary stalk interruption syndrome (PSIS) is a congenital structural defect characterised by a trio of thin or interrupted pituitary stalks, an absent or underdeveloped anterior pituitary gland and an absent or ectopic posterior pituitary gland. PSIS is an extremely rare syndrome, and its exact pathogenesis remains unclear. However, a combination of environmental factors such as perinatal complications—including breech delivery, obstructed labour, the use of forceps and caesarean delivery—may lead to pituitary stalk ischaemia. Conversely, genetic factors, including mutations in the Wingless and Int-1 (Wnt), Notch and Sonic Hedgehog (Shh) signalling pathways, specifically the PIT1, PROP1, LHX3/LHX4, PROKR2, OTX2, TGIF and HESX1 genes, play a significant role in the formation and development of the pituitary gland and its hormonal pathways. A positive family history of PSIS may contribute to the syndrome. A recent study hypothesised that the pathogenesis of PSIS results from a combined effect of mechanical forces during childbirth and congenital gene abnormalities. The reported incidence of PSIS is approximately 0.5 per 1 000 000 live births.Focal cortical dysplasia (FCD) is a developmental neuronal migration disorder of the cerebral cortex that leads to the disorganisation of cell architecture. Gene mutations in the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway can cause abnormal cell structure, function and disrupted cortical lamination, which can manifest as seizures that are resistant to medications. The prevalence of FCD ranges from 5% to 25%.PSIS is associated with a wide range of midline defects, including septo-optic dysplasia, characterised by septogenesis, agenesis of the corpus callosum, optic nerve hypoplasia, pituitary deficit and holoprosencephaly. Early detection, diagnosis and treatment are crucial to prevent future morbidities such as infertility, growth impairment, life-threatening pituitary crises and intellectual delay. Diagnosing patients with PSIS and FCD is essential for early seizure control using antiepileptic medications, which can improve their cognitive function and quality of life. This case report primarily aims to underscore the possible relationship between PSIS and structural brain abnormalities, such as FCD.

Case presentationA young boy in middle childhood presented to our hospital with a history of four episodes of generalised tonic-clonic convulsions, each lasting 1–2 min. These episodes were associated with facial cyanosis, eye-rolling, increased secretion, urinary incontinence and postictal drowsiness.His first seizure occurred 1 year prior to his visit. At that time, the seizure was observed to be afebrile and generalised tonic-clonic, lasting up to 2 min, also presenting with facial cyanosis, eye-rolling, increased secretion, urinary incontinence and postictal drowsiness. This initial seizure required hospitalisation and was managed with rectal diazepam. Following the first episode, the patient experienced three additional seizures, each lasting less than 1 min, with the same presentation, which did not necessitate hospitalisation.The patient had a weight gain of 12 kg in the previous 8 months. He denied a history of tiredness, cold intolerance, dry skin, muscle aches, headache, visual disturbances, loss of appetite, mood changes, loss of smell, change in sleeping pattern, change in bowel movement, nausea or vomiting and urinary symptoms such as frequency, urgency, burning sensation, polyuria and polydipsia.According to the mother, she had regular follow-ups with her obstetrician and an uneventful pregnancy. She complied with all antenatal visits, and ultrasonography was performed regularly, showing a normally growing foetus with a cephalic presentation. In terms of neonatal history, he was born at term via lower segment caesarean section (LSCS) due to an entangled cord and the maternal history of a previous LSCS. The delivery was without complications, and no postpartum complications were noted. The Apgar score was nine and nine at 1 min and 5 min after birth, respectively; his birth weight was 3 kg, which was on the 50th percentile, with a head circumference of 33 cm, on the 25th percentile and a length of 50 cm. There was no history of admission to the neonatal intensive care unit.Developmentally, his mother reported that he required more time to memorise and study, but his overall academic performance ranged between very good and excellent. His developmental milestones and cognitive functions were normal for his age. His parents are non-consanguineous; however, there is a family history of short stature and obesity on the maternal side, likely of familial type based on detailed family history. There was no documented family history of pathological endocrine disorders, neurological disorders or congenital disorders.His initial growth parameters were as follows: weight, 70 kg; height, 134 cm; body mass index, 38.4, classified as obese class II; maternal height, 157 cm; paternal height, 164 cm; mid-parental height, 167 cm. Additionally, his physical examination revealed a young boy with no dysmorphic features who was short and obese, with excessive skin folds, gynaecomastia, acanthosis nigricans over the nape of the neck and one café au lait spot on the right knee.Neurological examination showed normal deep tendon reflexes, tone, power and gait, with an intact cranial nerve examination, a negative Babinski sign and no cerebellar signs. The genital examination identified a buried micropenis measuring 3 cm in stretched penile length; the right testicle was 3 mL in volume and prepubertal, while the left testicle was cryptorchid. The rest of his systemic examination was unremarkable.Based on the patient’s clinical presentation, which included seizures, cryptorchidism, micropenis, obesity and short stature, pituitary gland pathology was suspected. Subsequently, a series of laboratory investigations were ordered, and a brain MRI was performed, which showed PSIS with FCD, explaining the seizure manifestations. Various differentials were considered, such as Kallmann syndrome due to the presence of cryptorchidism and a micropenis, but this was ruled out due to the absence of anosmia and specific MRI findings. Perinatal asphyxia was also considered, given the history of an entangled cord but was excluded based on the lack of neurological deficits, normal developmental milestones and no brain MRI findings. Hypothalamic hamartomas and septo-optic dysplasia were initially suspected but were later ruled out.

InvestigationsA routine blood investigation was conducted during his first seizure to rule out infection, electrolyte imbalances, hormonal deficiencies and other possible causes, which included a complete blood count (CBC), electrolytes and hormonal levels.Laboratory investigations revealed a sodium level of 136.7 mmol/L (normal range: 136–145) and a potassium level of 5.03 mmol/L (normal range: 3.5–5.1), with the rest of the electrolytes within the reference range, helping to rule out electrolyte imbalance-related seizures. The random blood glucose level was normal, ruling out hypoglycaemia-related convulsions; however, the insulin level was elevated at 33.26 mIU/mL (normal range: 2.6–24.9). Haemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) test and oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) were performed, both of which were normal. The prolactin level was within the normal range at 11 ng/mL (normal range: 4.04–15.2).In assessing the hypothalamic-pituitary-thyroid axis, the thyroid function test (TFT) showed low levels of free thyroxine at 7 pmol/L (normal range: 12–22), a thyroid-stimulating hormone level of 3.13 µIU/mL (normal range: 0.27–4.2) and free triiodothyronine at 4.65 pmol/L (normal range: 3.1–6.8), suggesting secondary hypothyroidism.In the assessment of the hypothalamic-pituitary-somatotropic axis, a growth hormone (GH) stimulation test indicated GH deficiency with a value of less than 0.05 ng/mL, and insulin-like growth factor 1 was low at 49.93 ng/mL (normal range: 186.5–424.5), suggesting GH deficiency.The hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis, adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) and dehydroepiandrosterone levels were deficient. Early morning cortisol levels and synacthen stimulation tests indicated an average level of cortisol.Antidiuretic hormone levels were within the normal range. Urine osmolality and specific gravity tests were performed and returned normal results, helping to rule out posterior pituitary involvement, specifically central diabetes insipidus.The lipid profile results included total cholesterol at 5.2 mmol/L (average<5.2), triglycerides at 1 mmol/L (average<1.7), low-density lipoprotein at 3.65 mmol/L (normal range: 0.0–2.59) and high-density lipoprotein at 1 mmol/L (normal range: 0.0–1.68).The laboratory findings indicate hypothyroidism, hypogonadism and ACTH deficiency, highly suggestive of multiple pituitary hormone deficiencies. This steered us towards hormonal replacement therapy as the main management plan.An MRI was performed to rule out possible anatomical defects related to the patient’s clinical presentation, revealing an absent pituitary stalk, hypoplasia of the anterior pituitary and ectopic or absent posterior pituitary gland (), which explain the micropenis, short stature, hypothyroidism, hypogonadism and ACTH deficiency.Moreover, a large area of markedly increased cortical thickness in the right parietal region, centred at the cortex of the Sylvian fissure and most pronounced in the posterior aspect, was highly suggestive of FCD (). Additionally, the FCD observed on the brain MRI is the most likely cause of the seizures.The laboratory findings showed normal levels of sodium and glucose, aligning with the fact that hyponatraemia often plays a significant role in seizures in PSIS, affirming that FCD is the cause of his seizures. Whole exome sequencing was conducted to identify potential genetic mutations, and the result was negative.

Differential diagnosisApproaching a case like the one portrayed here was challenging, especially since the initial clinical symptom was seizure. Based on a detailed history and physical examination, our top differentials are as follows:Kallmann syndrome was considered due to the presence of cryptorchidism and a micropenis, which are observed in patients with PSIS, but was ruled out because of the absence of anosmia and MRI findings of PSIS.Perinatal asphyxia was also considered due to a history of an entangled umbilical cord and a previous caesarean section; however, it was ruled out due to the lack of neurological deficits, normal developmental milestones and the absence of brain MRI findings indicating hypoxic insult.Hypothalamic hamartomas were considered as a differential diagnosis due to seizures and short stature, which are noted in patients with PSIS, but were ruled out because of the absence of gelastic seizures, precocious puberty, neurobehavioral symptoms and MRI findings of hamartomas.Septo-optic dysplasia was initially considered due to pituitary gland involvement and shared genetic aetiology commonly seen in patients with PSIS but was ruled out due to the absence of supportive brain MRI findings of septo-optic dysplasia.

TreatmentThe patient was started on thyroxine due to secondary hypothyroidism, as indicated by laboratory findings of low T4 levels. Thyroid function will be monitored through signs and symptoms, as well as TFTs, every 3 months to assess overall treatment response.Due to GH deficiency, indicated by the results of the GH stimulation test, he was started on GH therapy. The response to GH replacement therapy will be assessed by monitoring growth parameters every 3 months.For this patient, cortisol levels will be monitored through early morning cortisol testing, with watchful waiting every 3 months due to normal laboratory findings.Puberty induction will begin at 12 years of age, based on physical examination findings and laboratory results showing low follicle-stimulating hormone, luteinising hormone and testosterone levels. The overall response will be assessed by clinical examination.The patient was started on oxcarbazepine, which was prescribed by a paediatric neurologist. He began treatment at a dose of 300 mg (one tablet two times per day); however, the last seizure occurred around the same time as his weight gain, prompting an adjustment of his dosage based on ongoing weight gain—currently at 600 mg. Since that adjustment, the patient has been seizure-free and is closely monitored for further dosage adjustments and seizure activity.Due to hyperinsulinaemia, further investigations including HbA1c test and OGTT were conducted, which returned normal results. He was referred to a dietitian for advice on a well-balanced, calorie-controlled diet tailored to his age and activity level to facilitate weight loss and encourage regular physical activity to improve insulin sensitivity and assist with blood glucose monitoring. Consideration should be given to metformin, given its effectiveness in reducing insulin resistance in obese children.A psychologist will be involved to address the psychological effects of long-term chronic illness.Regular follow-up appointments will be scheduled with the endocrinology, neurology and dietitian clinics every 3 months.

Outcome and follow-upThe aim of starting early hormonal therapy is to prevent possible future complications such as intellectual disability, short stature, delayed puberty and infertility and to improve the quality of life.Recent results indicate that the patient is euthyroid; treatment response will be monitored based on signs and symptoms, physical examination and laboratory findings of TFT every 3 months.The effectiveness of GH replacement therapy will be determined by monitoring growth parameters. A recent visit showed a normal growth velocity according to his age within 6 months of starting treatment; this response will also be monitored regularly.Puberty induction will start at 12 years of age, with the outcome monitored through clinical examination.Cortisol levels will continue to be monitored every 3 months with watchful waiting, given the normal early morning cortisol levels.The patient has been seizure-free since the last dose adjustment and is regularly monitored for weight gain to facilitate possible dose adjustments every 3 months.In the context of hyperinsulinaemia, metformin should be considered due to its effectiveness in reducing insulin resistance in obese children.A psychologist will address the psychological impact of chronic illness and provide behavioural therapy for any developmental or behavioural issues arising from hormonal imbalances.Regular follow-up appointments with the endocrinologist, neurologist and dietitian will be scheduled every 3 months.

DiscussionThe pituitary gland secretes hormones that influence metabolism, blood pressure, growth and reproduction. PSIS has a documented incidence of 0.5 per million live births, with a male predominance and a sex ratio of 1.7. The pathophysiology of PSIS is unknown, but many hypotheses suggest that chromosomal microdeletions, including mutations in transcription factor genes such as HESX1 (3p21.2-p21.1), LHX4, SOX3, OTX2 and PROKR2, may play a role. A new hypothesis proposes that abnormal embryonic development, genetic background and specific genetic mutations contribute to an increased likelihood of delivery-related abnormalities, with varying degrees of traction during delivery, potentially leading to different levels of pituitary stalk interruption and an ectopic posterior lobe. Additionally, variants of genes linked to midline brain abnormalities are frequently found in PSIS, supporting the concept of a polygenic aetiology for this condition. Importantly, 5% of patients diagnosed with PSIS have a family history. Perinatal asphyxia may also impact the anterior pituitary gland, leading to stalk ischaemia and defective migration of the pituitary gland during intrauterine life. Clinical manifestations during the perinatal period can be severe and complex and may even be life-threatening. These manifestations include hypoglycaemia, prolonged jaundice, seizures, failure to thrive, recurrent hyponatraemia, poor sucking and congenital anomalies such as microphallus, cryptorchidism and patent ductus arteriosus.In childhood, symptoms may present as seizures, short stature, poor growth, delayed puberty, small penis and cryptorchidism. PSIS is diagnosed via MRI, typically revealing a triad of a hypoplastic or absent anterior pituitary gland, a thin or absent pituitary stalk and an ectopic posterior pituitary gland. The literature attributes the pathophysiology of seizures in PSIS to hypoglycaemia and hyponatraemia secondary to adrenal insufficiency. However, in our case, both sodium and glucose levels were normal, and the results of the synacthen test were within the normal range.PSIS is associated with a wide spectrum of midline anomalies, including septo-optic dysplasia, which encompasses septogenesis, agenesis of the corpus callosum, optic nerve hypoplasia, pituitary deficiency and holoprosencephaly. Holoprosencephaly results when the prosencephalon fails to separate properly between days 18 and 28 of gestation, leading to brain abnormalities, facial deformities (such as cyclopia, median or bilateral labial or palatal clefts, hypertelorism and single median incisors) and intellectual disabilities.In this case, we initially suspected a pituitary pathology based on the clinical presentation of afebrile seizures and the physical examination findings, which revealed a short stature, an obese boy, micropenis and left cryptorchidism, with a normal neurological examination suggesting an endocrine pathology. Laboratory investigations included a CBC, which was normal, along with normal electrolyte levels. However, TFTs showed abnormal findings suggestive of secondary hypothyroidism.A brain MRI confirmed PSIS with FCD. Subsequent measurements of posterior pituitary hormones showed normal values, while anterior pituitary hormones indicated panhypopituitarism. Therefore, with normal electrolyte levels, a normal CBC and MRI findings of PSIS and diffuse FCD, the most likely cause of his seizures is FCD, which can lead to drug-resistant epilepsy.The coexistence of FCD and PSIS may suggest a shared genetic mutation or a polygenic aetiology arising from gene expression at various stages of embryonic development or related to a common pathway injury. This case report primarily aims to highlight the potential link between PSIS and structural brain abnormalities, such as FCD, paving the way for future research to explore this uncommon relationship, identify possible common pathogenic gene variants and determine the prevalence of pituitary pathology in conjunction with cortical malformations.Learning pointsHighlight the potential link between cortical malformations, such as focal cortical dysplasia, and pituitary pathology, such as pituitary stalk interruption syndrome (PSIS), emphasising the need for future research to confirm this unusual relationship.Stress the rare but significant possibilities of cortical malformations and pituitary conditions, such as PSIS, as causes for seizures, as discussed in this case report.Emphasise that the quality of life and prognosis for patients with PSIS can be improved through early identification of hormonal deficiencies and timely initiation of treatment, thereby minimising future issues, such as delayed puberty, infertility, short stature, intellectual disability and enhancing the overall quality of life.Underline the importance of thorough history taking, a comprehensive physical examination (including growth parameters) and MRI as the gold standard modality for evaluating patients with suspected neuroendocrine malformations.
