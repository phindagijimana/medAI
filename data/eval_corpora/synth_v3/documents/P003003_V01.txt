Assessment / Impression

This patient has drug-resistant epilepsy beginning at age 11, with generalized tonic-clonic seizures currently occurring approximately 7 times per week. The epilepsy has been categorized as generalized, and the reported etiology is structural based on MRI evidence of focal cortical dysplasia. Current antiseizure medications are oxcarbazepine and valproate.

The high ongoing seizure burden despite current therapy is concerning. A key issue is the apparent discordance between the reported generalized epilepsy classification and the focal structural lesion. Focal cortical dysplasia is potentially relevant to seizure onset, but the available information is insufficient to determine whether it is the epileptogenic substrate, an incidental imaging finding, or part of a more complex electroclinical network. Likewise, without review of prior EEG data or capture of habitual events, I cannot confirm whether the convulsions are generalized at onset or focal to bilateral.

The patient warrants comprehensive diagnostic characterization. If seizures are shown to arise consistently from the region of focal cortical dysplasia, the patient may be considered for a formal surgical epilepsy evaluation. If the electroclinical data instead support generalized epilepsy without a focal onset, treatment planning would need to reflect that classification. Medication reconciliation is also essential, particularly because doses and frequencies are currently unavailable.

Recommendations / Plan

1. We will obtain and review the original brain MRI images and the complete radiology report documenting focal cortical dysplasia. If the available study is not adequate for epilepsy-focused review, an epilepsy-protocol brain MRI may be considered.

2. Obtain prior routine EEG, ambulatory EEG, and any previous inpatient VEEG or EMU records. No EEG findings were available in the referral material.

3. Recommend inpatient EMU admission for LTM/VEEG to capture the habitual generalized tonic-clonic events, define ictal onset, assess for focal versus generalized interictal epileptiform discharges, and determine whether the MRI lesion is electroclinically concordant.

4. Continue oxcarbazepine and valproate without a documented dose change pending complete medication reconciliation. The patient should provide medication bottles or an updated pharmacy list confirming formulation, dose, frequency, adherence, and prescriber instructions.

5. Following electroclinical characterization and imaging review, discuss candidacy for a multidisciplinary surgical epilepsy evaluation. Additional presurgical testing would depend on whether a reproducible focal seizure onset is demonstrated.

6. The treatment plan should be reassessed after EMU data and MRI review are available. At that time, we can determine whether the current classification of generalized epilepsy remains appropriate and whether medication optimization, surgical evaluation, or another advanced epilepsy therapy should be pursued.

7. Standard seizure safety counseling should be reinforced, including avoidance of potentially dangerous activities when a seizure could result in injury and adherence to applicable NY driving restrictions.
