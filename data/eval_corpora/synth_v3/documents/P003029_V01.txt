Assessment / Impression

This is a 66-year-old patient with seizure onset at age 66 and a reported seizure frequency of approximately three per month. The described seizure type is typical absence, and the epilepsy has been classified as generalized. The presumed etiology is structural based on a brain MRI demonstrating a tumor.

The patient is currently taking oxcarbazepine and has been characterized as drug responsive. On the information presently available, this does not meet a clear description of drug-resistant epilepsy. Nevertheless, the patient continues to have seizures, and the exact degree of response cannot be assessed without the medication dose, baseline seizure burden, and longitudinal treatment history.

There are important points requiring clarification. Typical absence seizures are generalized seizures, while a focal structural lesion may produce focal seizures that can be clinically subtle. The reported tumor may still be causally related, but direct review of the MRI and EEG is needed to reconcile the electroclinical and imaging information. I would avoid assuming either a generalized genetic syndrome or a focal tumor-associated epilepsy until the prior studies are obtained or repeated.

Oxcarbazepine may be effective for focal-onset epilepsy, but sodium-channel agents can aggravate some generalized seizure types, including absence seizures. I therefore would not escalate or abruptly discontinue oxcarbazepine solely on the basis of the limited information currently available. Any medication adjustment should follow diagnostic confirmation and verification of the present dose.

Recommendations / Plan

1. Continue Trileptal (oxcarbazepine) at the currently prescribed regimen for now. The patient should not stop this medication abruptly. We will confirm the tablet strength, dose, frequency, adherence, and prescribing pharmacy through formal medication reconciliation.

2. Obtain the prior MRI brain images and complete radiology report for direct review at xxx Medical Center. If the previous study was not performed with an appropriate brain tumor or epilepsy protocol, updated MRI brain imaging with and without contrast should be considered.

3. Obtain all prior EEG reports and, if possible, the original EEG data. If no adequate study exists, I recommend a routine EEG to assess for generalized spike-wave activity, focal IEDs, or other evidence that may clarify seizure onset.

4. If the routine EEG and clinical history remain discordant, consider prolonged ambulatory EEG or inpatient VEEG/LTM through the EMU to capture and characterize the reported absence events.

5. Additional management of the MRI-identified tumor should be based on image review. Depending on the lesion’s location and radiographic characteristics, referral to neurosurgery and/or neuro-oncology may be appropriate. The current information is insufficient to determine resectability or epilepsy surgical candidacy.

6. With ongoing oxcarbazepine therapy, routine monitoring should include a CBC and comprehensive metabolic panel, including serum sodium. Medication levels may be considered when clinically useful after the dose is confirmed.

7. The patient should follow standard seizure precautions and applicable NY driving restrictions while seizures continue. Avoid unsupervised swimming, heights, open flames, and operation of dangerous machinery. Seizure first-aid counseling was recommended.

8. Follow-up in the epilepsy clinic after the MRI and EEG materials are available, or sooner for increased seizure frequency, prolonged events, medication intolerance, or another clinical concern.
