Indication: Evaluation of generalized epilepsy with typical absence seizures. Seizure onset was at age 75 years, with an unknown etiology. Events currently occur approximately four times per month and are described as brief behavioral arrest with impaired responsiveness. The epilepsy has been drug responsive. Current antiseizure medication is brivaracetam.

Technique: A 32-minute digital EEG was performed using the international 10-20 system with 21 scalp electrodes and a single-lead ECG. The study included synchronized video. Standard activation procedures included hyperventilation for 3 minutes and intermittent photic stimulation. The patient was recorded during wakefulness and drowsiness. Stage N2 sleep was achieved.

Description:

Background: The waking background was continuous, symmetric, and appropriately organized, with a 9 Hz posterior dominant rhythm that was reactive to eye opening and closure. An expected anterior-to-posterior gradient was present. Low-amplitude beta activity was seen anteriorly. There was no persistent focal slowing or significant interhemispheric asymmetry.

Awake State: During wakefulness, there were occasional generalized, frontally predominant 3- to 3.5-Hz spike-and-wave and polyspike-and-wave discharges. These were bilaterally synchronous and symmetric, maximal over Fp1/Fp2 and F3/F4, without a consistent focal lead-in. Most bursts lasted 1-2 seconds and had no definite clinical correlate.

Drowsy/Sleep: With drowsiness, the PDR attenuated and diffuse theta activity emerged. Stage N2 sleep was characterized by symmetric vertex waves, sleep spindles, and K complexes. Generalized spike-and-wave discharges were mildly activated by drowsiness and sleep. No focal IEDs, LPDs, GPDs, or TIRDA were present.

Hyperventilation: Hyperventilation produced expected diffuse theta slowing and activated a 7-second burst of generalized 3-Hz spike-and-wave activity. On video, the patient stopped responding to the technologist and paused an ongoing counting task, with immediate return to baseline at discharge termination. There was no postictal confusion. This event was electroclinically consistent with a typical absence seizure.

Photic Stimulation: Photic stimulation at frequencies from 1 to 20 Hz produced symmetric photic driving at several frequencies. There was no photoparoxysmal response.

Interictal Findings: Occasional generalized, frontally predominant 3- to 3.5-Hz spike-and-wave and polyspike-and-wave discharges, increased during drowsiness, sleep, and hyperventilation.

Ictal Events: One electroclinical typical absence seizure was captured during hyperventilation, consisting of abrupt behavioral arrest and impaired responsiveness associated with generalized 3-Hz spike-and-wave activity lasting 7 seconds. Electrographic onset and offset were abrupt, without focal features or postictal slowing.

Impression:

1. Abnormal routine EEG due to occasional generalized 3- to 3.5-Hz spike-and-wave and polyspike-and-wave discharges, indicating increased epileptic potential in a generalized distribution.
2. One typical absence seizure was elicited by hyperventilation, with generalized 3-Hz spike-and-wave activity and brief behavioral arrest.
3. In the appropriate clinical context, these findings support a diagnosis of generalized epilepsy with typical absence seizures. No focal epileptiform abnormality or focal cerebral dysfunction was identified.
