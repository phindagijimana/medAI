History of Present Illness:  
The patient is a 41-year-old right-handed man with drug-resistant epilepsy of unknown etiology. Seizures began at approximately age 30. His established habitual events are generalized tonic-clonic seizures, described as convulsive episodes with loss of awareness, generalized stiffening and shaking, and subsequent post-ictal confusion. Associated lateral tongue biting may occur. Available history does not clearly establish a preceding focal aura, focal impaired awareness phase, consistent versive movement, or other reproducible lateralizing feature. There is no reliable history available at admission of separate absence seizures or myoclonic seizures.

Despite ongoing treatment with multiple antiseizure medications, he continues to have breakthrough generalized tonic-clonic seizures at an average frequency of approximately one per year. Although the absolute event frequency is low, the events carry substantial risk because of abrupt loss of awareness, convulsion, injury potential, and post-ictal impairment. No interval seizure events were documented between the most recent outpatient assessment and this admission. Specific recent provoking factors, including missed medication, sleep deprivation, alcohol exposure, or acute illness, have not been established.

His current outpatient regimen includes Keppra (levetiracetam), Briviact (brivaracetam), and Trileptal (oxcarbazepine). Exact outpatient doses and timing are being verified through medication reconciliation and the pharmacy record. The concurrent use of two SV2A agents will also be confirmed with the patient and outpatient prescriber. Prior medication trials, maximum tolerated doses, treatment duration, adverse effects, and reasons for discontinuation are not fully available in the admission records. Nevertheless, continued convulsive seizures despite a multi-drug regimen supports drug-resistant epilepsy.

The current working classification is generalized epilepsy with generalized tonic-clonic seizures, although the etiology remains unknown. He is admitted for phase I VEEG evaluation to document interictal and ictal findings and to determine whether the EEG supports generalized onset or identifies an unrecognized focal onset with rapid bilateral synchronization. This distinction is important for medication selection and advanced therapy planning. If generalized onset is confirmed, resective surgery would generally not be appropriate; neuromodulation options may be considered depending on the full electroclinical data. Results will be reviewed in a multidisciplinary patient review conference after completion of the necessary testing.
