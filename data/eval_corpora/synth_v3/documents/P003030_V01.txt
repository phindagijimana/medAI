Assessment / Impression

This is an adult patient with reported generalized epilepsy characterized by generalized tonic-clonic seizures beginning at age 36. The current reported seizure burden is approximately five per month. The epilepsy is described as having an infectious etiology and as drug responsive. Current ASMs are zonisamide and brivaracetam.

At present, the available information supports the referring diagnosis but does not provide sufficient detail to independently verify the generalized classification or the proposed infectious etiology. In particular, no EEG or MRI data were available for direct review. The basis for labeling the epilepsy drug responsive also requires clarification given the reported frequency of five seizures per month. This does not necessarily represent a contradiction, as the frequency may reflect a prior period or a substantial response from a higher baseline; however, the available record does not establish that timeline.

Medication doses and frequencies were not supplied, and the prior ASM history is unknown. For that reason, I would not make an empiric dose adjustment from this limited documentation. A complete medication reconciliation and review of prior response, adherence, adverse effects, and seizure chronology are necessary before determining whether the present regimen should be continued unchanged or optimized.

Recommendations / Plan

1. Continue the current zonisamide and brivaracetam regimen without an undocumented dose change pending confirmation of the actual doses and administration schedule.

2. Obtain a complete medication reconciliation, including pharmacy-confirmed zonisamide and brivaracetam doses, frequency of administration, adherence, tolerability, and duration of treatment.

3. Request the prior neurology records documenting seizure onset at age 36, the generalized epilepsy classification, and the infectious etiology. Any hospitalization or infectious disease records relevant to the presumed cause would also be helpful.

4. Request prior EEG and neuroimaging reports and, when available, the original studies for direct review. The generalized classification should be correlated with the electroclinical data rather than inferred from convulsive semiology alone.

5. Clarify the seizure frequency with a prospective seizure diary. The patient should document generalized tonic-clonic events, including approximate duration, clustering, possible triggers, postictal recovery, and missed medication doses, if any.

6. Clarify whether the reported frequency of five per month is current or historical and determine the degree of response to zonisamide and brivaracetam. The designation of drug-responsive epilepsy should be reassessed after this timeline is established.

7. We reviewed standard seizure precautions. The patient should avoid unsupervised swimming, bathing alone, heights, open flames, and operation of dangerous machinery while seizures remain active. Driving should follow applicable NY requirements and clinician guidance.

8. The patient was advised not to stop either ASM abruptly. Any medication change should be supervised because abrupt withdrawal may worsen seizure control.

9. Follow up in the epilepsy clinic after the outside records, medication details, and seizure diary are available. At that visit, we can determine whether additional diagnostic evaluation or medication optimization is appropriate.
