Ordering Provider: Dr. xxx  
Indication: Focal epilepsy with focal impaired-awareness seizures beginning at age 16, currently occurring approximately once monthly. Structural etiology is suspected based on MRI evidence of hippocampal sclerosis. The epilepsy has been drug responsive. Current antiseizure medications are oxcarbazepine and lamotrigine. EEG requested to assess for interictal epileptiform activity.

Technique: A 30-minute digital EEG was performed using the international 10-20 system with 21 scalp electrodes and a single-lead electrocardiogram. The study included referential and bipolar montages. Standard activation procedures included hyperventilation for 3 minutes and intermittent photic stimulation. The patient was recorded during wakefulness and drowsiness. Stage N2 sleep was achieved.

Description:

Background: During maximal wakefulness, there was a symmetric, well-organized 9-10 Hz posterior dominant rhythm of moderate amplitude that attenuated appropriately with eye opening. An anterior-to-posterior gradient was present. Low-amplitude beta activity was seen over the frontocentral regions. There was no persistent focal asymmetry or generalized background slowing.

Awake state: The awake background was continuous and reactive. No focal slowing, lateralized periodic discharges, generalized periodic discharges, or other rhythmic or periodic patterns were identified.

Drowsy/sleep: With drowsiness, there was attenuation of the PDR and emergence of diffuse theta activity with slow roving eye movements. Stage N2 sleep was characterized by symmetric vertex waves, sleep spindles, and K complexes. No epileptiform abnormalities were activated by sleep.

Hyperventilation: Hyperventilation produced mild, symmetric generalized theta slowing without focal or epileptiform activation. The background returned promptly to baseline after completion of the procedure.

Photic stimulation: Intermittent photic stimulation at frequencies from 1 to 30 Hz produced symmetric posterior driving responses at several flash frequencies. There was no photoparoxysmal response.

Interictal findings: No definite spikes, sharp waves, spike-and-wave discharges, TIRDA, or other interictal epileptiform discharges were observed. No electrographic evidence of focal cerebral dysfunction was present during this recording.

Ictal events: No electrographic seizures or clinical events were captured. The single-lead ECG demonstrated a regular rhythm.

Impression:

1. Normal routine EEG during wakefulness, drowsiness, and stage N2 sleep.  
2. No focal slowing, interictal epileptiform discharges, or electrographic seizures were identified.

A normal interictal EEG does not exclude a clinical diagnosis of focal epilepsy. The patient’s history remains consistent with focal epilepsy manifesting as focal impaired-awareness seizures, with a structural etiology supported by MRI-demonstrated hippocampal sclerosis. Clinical correlation is recommended, including consideration of prolonged video EEG monitoring if further electroclinical characterization is required despite treatment with oxcarbazepine and lamotrigine.
