Referral Question:  
Diagnostic and presurgical neuropsychological baseline to characterize cognitive functioning in the context of focal epilepsy and hippocampal sclerosis, assess temporal-lobe-mediated memory and language functions, and inform multidisciplinary discussion at PRC.

Relevant History:  
The patient has a h/o focal epilepsy with onset at age 11. Typical events have been classified as focal impaired-awareness seizures. The patient denies tongue biting. Current seizure frequency is 0/month, with the patient presently seizure-free on antiseizure medication. The epilepsy is considered structurally mediated in the setting of MRI-demonstrated hippocampal sclerosis and is currently drug responsive.

Current ASMs include levetiracetam and perampanel; doses were not available for review. Brivaracetam was previously trialed and discontinued because of cognitive side effects. Available records also describe some events as possibly suggestive of a PNES component. Accordingly, if atypical spells recur, electroclinical correlation would be helpful to r/o nonepileptic events. EEG findings and the laterality of the hippocampal sclerosis were not available in the materials reviewed.

Detailed developmental, educational, occupational, psychiatric, substance-use, and family histories were not available and should be integrated into final clinical decision-making. No conclusions regarding premorbid academic functioning or language dominance can be made solely from the available history.

Behavioral Observations:

Arrived appropriately dressed and groomed. In NAD. Alert and oriented. Cooperative throughout the evaluation.

Speech and Language:  
Speech fluent, grammatical, and normally prosodic. No paraphasic errors in conversation. Comprehension adequate for multistep instructions.

Mood and Affect:  
Mood described as generally stable. Affect appropriate and congruent. No overt behavioral evidence of severe depression, anxiety, psychosis, or mania.

Effort and Engagement:  
Consistent task persistence. Embedded performance-validity indices were within expectation. TOMM Trial 2 = 50/50, indicating adequate engagement. Results are considered a valid estimate of current cognitive functioning.

Tests Administered:

Results:

Intellectual Functioning:  
WAIS-IV Full Scale IQ was Average (FSIQ=94, 34th percentile). Verbal Comprehension was Average (VCI=93, 32nd percentile), as was Perceptual Reasoning (PRI=100, 50th percentile). Working Memory was Average (WMI=92, 30th percentile). Processing Speed was Low Average (PSI=89, 23rd percentile). There was no evidence of generalized intellectual impairment.

Attention/Processing Speed:  
Auditory attention and working memory ranged from Low Average to Average (Digit Span scaled score=8; Arithmetic scaled score=9). Visual scanning and psychomotor sequencing were Low Average on Trail Making A (T=41). WAIS-IV Coding was Low Average (scaled score=7), while Symbol Search was Average (scaled score=9). The pattern suggests mild inefficiency rather than a primary attention disorder.

Language:  
Confrontation naming was Low Average on the Boston Naming Test (T=41), with benefit from phonemic cueing. Letter fluency was Low Average (FAS T=40), whereas semantic fluency was Average (Animals T=47). Verbal abstraction was Average. The mild naming weakness may reflect temporal-language network inefficiency, although it does not independently establish hemispheric dominance or lesion laterality.

Verbal Memory:  
On the CVLT-3, acquisition across five learning trials was Low Average (Trials 1-5 standard score=85, 16th percentile). Learning improved with repetition, though the acquisition slope was somewhat shallow. Short-delay free recall was Borderline (scaled score=6), and long-delay free recall was Borderline (scaled score=5). Recognition discriminability was Low Average, with benefit from recognition cues but several false-positive errors.

WMS-IV Logical Memory immediate recall was Low Average (scaled score=7), and delayed recall was Borderline (scaled score=6). Recognition was Low Average. Collectively, verbal memory was weaker than overall intellectual ability, with inefficient learning and reduced delayed retrieval. Retention was not uniformly amnestic, given partial benefit from cueing and recognition.

Visual Memory:  
WMS-IV Visual Reproduction immediate and delayed recall were Average (scaled scores=10 and 9, respectively). ROCF delayed recall was Average (T=48), with adequate retention of the original figure organization. Visual recognition was also within normal limits. Visual memory represented a relative strength compared with verbal memory.

Executive Function:  
Trail Making B was Low Average (T=40), without set-loss errors. Stroop inhibition was Average (T=46). On the WCST, the patient completed an expected number of categories, with perseverative responses in the Average range (T=47). Findings do not indicate a major executive-function disorder, although speeded cognitive flexibility was mildly inefficient.

Visuospatial Functioning:  
WAIS-IV Block Design was Average (scaled score=10). ROCF copy was within normal limits, with an organized approach and no evidence of visuospatial distortion or neglect.

Motor Functioning:  
Grooved Pegboard performance was Low Average bilaterally, without a clinically meaningful lateralized discrepancy. No gross motor abnormality was observed during testing.

Mood/Personality:  
BDI-II and BAI scores fell within the minimal range. Self-report did not indicate a current major depressive or anxiety syndrome. These measures do not resolve the question of a possible PNES component; diagnosis would require integration of event phenomenology, psychiatric assessment, and video-EEG data.

Summary and Impressions:  
This evaluation demonstrated broadly Average intellectual functioning, intact visuospatial abilities, preserved visual memory, and generally intact executive functioning. Relative weaknesses were observed in processing speed, confrontation naming, verbal learning, and delayed verbal recall. Performance validity was adequate, and the results are considered interpretable.

The verbal-greater-than-visual memory discrepancy is compatible with dysfunction involving language-mediated temporal memory systems and may be consistent with the known structural finding of hippocampal sclerosis. However, the available information does not specify MRI laterality, EEG localization, or language dominance. Therefore, the neuropsychological findings should not be used in isolation to lateralize seizure onset. The possible PNES component also remains clinically relevant, particularly if recurrent spells differ from the established focal impaired-awareness seizures.

Given current seizure freedom and drug-responsive status on levetiracetam and perampanel, the immediate risk-benefit balance for resective surgery differs from that of active drug-resistant epilepsy. If surgery is reconsidered following recurrence, intervention involving the language-dominant mesial temporal region could carry meaningful risk for additional verbal memory and naming decline. Conversely, the preserved visual-memory profile provides a useful baseline but does not by itself determine right temporal integrity.

Recommendations:  
1. Review findings at PRC with MRI laterality, scalp/video-EEG data, seizure semiology, and medication history.  
2. Continue neurologic follow-up and current ASMs as directed by Epileptology.  
3. If resective or ablative temporal surgery is considered, obtain task-based fMRI for language and memory lateralization. Consider Wada testing if fMRI is inconclusive or discordant.  
4. If atypical breakthrough events recur, consider prolonged video-EEG monitoring to characterize events and assess the possible PNES component.  
5. Should seizures become drug resistant, PRC may consider resection/ablation or neuromodulation options such as the NeuroPace RNS System, guided by localization and functional risk.  
6. Use written schedules, repetition, semantic organization, and external reminders to compensate for verbal-memory inefficiency.  
7. Repeat neuropsychological evaluation following any surgical intervention, preferably at approximately 6-12 months, or sooner if a significant cognitive change occurs.
