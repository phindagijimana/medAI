**Referral Question:**  
Diagnostic/presurgical neuropsychological baseline in the context of focal epilepsy and MRI-confirmed hippocampal sclerosis. Assess cognitive strengths and weaknesses, memory and language functioning, and potential lateralizing information for discussion at PRC.

**Relevant History:**  
The patient has a h/o focal epilepsy beginning at age 10, with focal-to-bilateral tonic-clonic seizures occurring approximately twice per year. Tongue biting is denied. There is no h/o CNS infection. The epilepsy is currently considered drug responsive. Current antiseizure medication is brivaracetam; dosing information was not available for review. If intolerable side effects arise, the treating team may consider cross-titration to lacosamide.

Brain MRI demonstrated hippocampal sclerosis. The focal MRI lesion is probably epileptogenic, although neuropsychological findings must be integrated with ictal EEG, seizure semiology, and other functional studies before definitive localization. EEG findings were not available for this evaluation. No prior epilepsy surgery was reported in the available records.

Detailed psychiatric, developmental, educational, and occupational histories were not available. Family history is notable for stroke in the patient’s mother. This evaluation was requested to characterize current functioning and provide baseline data should surgical or neuromodulation options later be considered.

**Behavioral Observations:**

**Appearance and Cooperation:**  
Arrived appropriately groomed. In NAD. Alert and oriented x3. Cooperative throughout the interview and testing.

**Speech and Language:**  
Speech fluent and normally articulated. No paraphasic errors in conversation. Comprehension adequate for multistep instructions.

**Mood and Affect:**  
Mood appeared euthymic. Affect appropriate and stable. No overt behavioral evidence of severe depression, anxiety, or psychosis.

**Effort and Engagement:**  
Good persistence across the evaluation. Embedded validity indices were within expectation. TOMM Trial 2 = 50/50, supporting adequate engagement. Results are considered a valid estimate of current cognitive functioning.

**Tests Administered:**  
WAIS-IV; Trail Making Test Parts A and B; Boston Naming Test; COWAT letter and animal fluency; CVLT-3; WMS-IV Logical Memory and Visual Reproduction; Rey-Osterrieth Complex Figure Test; Wisconsin Card Sorting Test; Stroop Color-Word Test; Grooved Pegboard; Beck Depression Inventory-II; Beck Anxiety Inventory; and Test of Memory Malingering.

**Results:**

**Intellectual Functioning:**  
WAIS-IV Full Scale IQ was in the Average range (FSIQ = 96, 39th percentile). Verbal Comprehension was Average (VCI = 98, 45th percentile), as was Perceptual Reasoning (PRI = 100, 50th percentile). Working Memory fell in the Low Average range (WMI = 89, 23rd percentile), while Processing Speed was Average (PSI = 97, 42nd percentile). The profile did not indicate generalized intellectual decline.

**Attention/Processing Speed:**  
Auditory attention and mental manipulation ranged from Low Average to Average (Digit Span scaled score = 8; Arithmetic scaled score = 9). Visual scanning and psychomotor sequencing were Average on Trail Making A (T = 48). WAIS-IV Coding and Symbol Search were also Average (scaled scores = 9 and 10, respectively). There was no evidence of clinically significant processing-speed impairment.

**Language:**  
Confrontation naming was Low Average on the Boston Naming Test (T = 41), with benefit from phonemic cues. Letter fluency was Average (COWAT FAS T = 47), and semantic fluency was Low Average (animals T = 42). Verbal abstraction was Average (Similarities scaled score = 10). Mild inefficiency in confrontation naming and semantic retrieval may reflect temporal-system dysfunction, although the magnitude of weakness is insufficient to establish hemispheric language dominance or confidently lateralize seizure onset.

**Verbal Memory:**  
On the CVLT-3, initial acquisition was Low Average, with modest improvement across learning trials (Trials 1–5 total scaled score = 7). Short-delay free recall was Borderline (scaled score = 5), and long-delay free recall was Low Average (scaled score = 6). Recognition discriminability was Low Average, with several false-positive errors. WMS-IV Logical Memory immediate recall was Average (scaled score = 9), while delayed recall declined to Low Average (scaled score = 7). Retention was mildly reduced but not amnestic. Overall, verbal memory reflected inefficient learning and retrieval with relatively better performance for structured contextual material.

**Visual Memory:**  
ROCF copy was intact (32/36), indicating adequate visuoconstruction. Immediate and delayed recall were Low Average (T = 42 and T = 40, respectively), with preserved overall configuration but reduced detail. WMS-IV Visual Reproduction immediate recall was Average (scaled score = 9), and delayed recall was Low Average (scaled score = 7). Recognition was broadly intact. Visual memory was mildly inefficient and generally comparable to verbal memory, yielding no compelling material-specific lateralizing pattern.

**Executive Function:**  
Trail Making B was Average (T = 46), without set-loss errors. Stroop inhibition was Low Average (T = 43). On the WCST, the patient completed five categories, with perseverative responses in the Average range (T = 48). Executive functioning was broadly preserved, with mild variability in inhibitory control but no evidence of a primary dysexecutive syndrome.

**Visuospatial Functioning:**  
Block Design was Average (scaled score = 10). ROCF copy was organized and accurate. No hemispatial neglect, constructional apraxia, or significant perceptual disturbance was observed.

**Motor Functioning:**  
Grooved Pegboard performance was Low Average bilaterally (dominant hand T = 42; nondominant hand T = 40). There was no meaningful lateralized motor discrepancy.

**Mood/Personality:**  
BDI-II and BAI scores fell within minimal symptom ranges. Self-report did not suggest that acute emotional distress substantially affected performance. Continued monitoring remains appropriate given the psychosocial burden associated with epilepsy.

**Summary and Impressions:**  
The evaluation documents Average overall intellectual ability with intact visuospatial skills, processing speed, and most executive functions. Relative weaknesses were observed in working memory, confrontation naming, semantic retrieval, and delayed recall of both verbal and visual information. Memory findings reflected inefficient acquisition and retrieval rather than rapid forgetting or a dense amnestic syndrome.

The mild, bilateral pattern of memory inefficiency is broadly compatible with temporal-system vulnerability in the setting of focal epilepsy and hippocampal sclerosis. However, the current profile is not strongly lateralizing. The MRI lesion is likely epileptogenic, but concordance with video-EEG, semiology, and functional language/memory mapping is necessary. Given drug-responsive epilepsy and a frequency of approximately two seizures per year, the relative benefits and risks of invasive treatment require multidisciplinary review. From a cognitive standpoint, resection involving the language-dominant mesial temporal region could carry risk for further verbal learning and memory decline.

**Recommendations:**

1. Discuss findings at PRC with integration of MRI, seizure semiology, and ictal/interictal EEG data.  
2. Obtain fMRI language mapping if temporal surgery is under active consideration. Consider Wada testing if fMRI is inconclusive or discordant.  
3. If intolerable brivaracetam side effects arise, cross-titrate to lacosamide per the treating epileptologist.  
4. If resection is not appropriate, PRC may consider neuromodulation options, including the NeuroPace RNS System, based on localization and overall clinical indications.  
5. Use external memory supports, including calendars, alarms, written instructions, and spaced review of newly learned information.  
6. Repeat neuropsychological evaluation 6–12 months s/p any surgical intervention, or earlier if there is a meaningful cognitive change.
