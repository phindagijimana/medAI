Referral Question:  
Diagnostic/presurgical neuropsychological evaluation to characterize current cognitive functioning in the context of focal epilepsy and hippocampal sclerosis, establish a cognitive baseline, and assist multidisciplinary review at PRC.

Relevant History:  
The patient has a hx of focal epilepsy beginning at age 6. Current events are described as focal impaired-awareness seizures, occurring approximately four times per month. Etiology is considered structural. MRI reportedly demonstrates hippocampal sclerosis; laterality was not specified in the records available for this evaluation. EEG localization data were not available for review.

The epilepsy is classified in the available record as drug-responsive. Current ASM is zonisamide; dose was not provided. The patient previously trialed brivaracetam, which was discontinued due to mood side effects. No other prior ASM trials were documented. Family h/o is notable for a sister with epilepsy.

Educational, occupational, psychiatric, developmental, and substance-use histories were not sufficiently detailed in the available source materials. These limitations should be considered when interpreting normative comparisons and estimating premorbid functioning.

Behavioral Observations:

Appearance and Cooperation:  
Arrived appropriately groomed. In NAD. Alert and oriented. Cooperative throughout interview and testing.

Speech and Language:  
Speech fluent and normally articulated. No obvious paraphasic errors during conversation. Comprehension adequate for multistep test instructions.

Mood and Affect:  
Affect generally appropriate to content. No marked behavioral dysregulation observed.

Effort and Engagement:  
Task persistence and frustration tolerance adequate. Performance on the Test of Memory Malingering was within expectation (TOMM Trial 2 = 50/50), supporting valid engagement. Embedded validity indices were also acceptable. Results are considered a reasonable estimate of current cognitive functioning.

Tests Administered:  
WAIS-IV; Trail Making Test Parts A and B; Boston Naming Test; COWAT letter and animal fluency; CVLT-3; WMS-IV Logical Memory and Visual Reproduction; Rey-Osterrieth Complex Figure; Wisconsin Card Sorting Test; Stroop Color-Word Test; Grooved Pegboard; Beck Depression Inventory-II; Beck Anxiety Inventory; TOMM.

Results:

Intellectual Functioning:  
Overall intellectual functioning fell in the Average range (WAIS-IV Full Scale IQ = 94, 34th percentile). Verbal Comprehension was Average (VCI = 98, 45th percentile), as was Perceptual Reasoning (PRI = 96, 39th percentile). Working Memory was Low Average (WMI = 86, 18th percentile), while Processing Speed was Average (PSI = 94, 34th percentile). The profile suggests broadly preserved general reasoning with relative inefficiency in auditory working memory.

Attention/Processing Speed:  
Basic auditory attention was Low Average to Average (Digit Span scaled score = 8). Visual scanning and sequencing were Average on Trail Making A (T = 47). Timed symbol transcription and visual discrimination were Average overall. Performance was accurate, without evidence of clinically significant inattention or impulsivity.

Language:  
Confrontation naming was Low Average (Boston Naming Test T = 42), with benefit from phonemic cueing. Letter fluency was Low Average (FAS T = 41), whereas semantic fluency was Average (animals T = 48). Verbal abstraction and receptive language were intact. The mild naming inefficiency may be relevant to temporal-system functioning, although it is not sufficiently specific to establish language dominance or seizure laterality.

Verbal Memory:  
On the CVLT-3, initial acquisition was Low Average, with a shallow but positive learning curve across trials (Trials 1-5 standard score = 84, 14th percentile). Short-delay free recall was Borderline (scaled score = 5), and long-delay free recall was Low Average (scaled score = 7). Recognition discrimination was Low Average, with several false-positive errors. WMS-IV Logical Memory immediate recall was Average (scaled score = 9), while delayed recall was Low Average (scaled score = 7). Retention improved when verbal information was organized and contextually meaningful. Overall, findings indicate mild weakness in verbal list learning and retrieval rather than a dense amnestic syndrome.

Visual Memory:  
Visual Reproduction immediate recall was Average (scaled score = 10), with Low Average delayed recall (scaled score = 7). ROCF delayed recall was Borderline (T = 36), partly reflecting an inefficient initial organizational approach. Recognition was stronger than free recall. This pattern suggests reduced spontaneous retrieval and organizational encoding rather than rapid loss of visual information.

Executive Function:  
Mental flexibility on Trail Making B was Low Average (T = 41). Stroop inhibition was Average (T = 46). On the WCST, concept formation was broadly intact, though there were mild inefficiencies in shifting response set (perseverative responses T = 42). Executive findings indicate subtle variability in cognitive flexibility without a pervasive frontal-system deficit.

Visuospatial Functioning:  
Block Design was Average (scaled score = 10). ROCF copy was intact in overall accuracy but mildly disorganized in approach. There was no evidence of spatial neglect, constructional apraxia, or a primary visuoperceptual disorder.

Motor Functioning:  
Fine-motor dexterity was Low Average bilaterally on the Grooved Pegboard, without a clinically meaningful lateralized discrepancy. No motor findings strongly suggested unilateral dysfunction.

Mood/Personality:  
Self-report measures indicated minimal depressive symptoms (BDI-II = 8) and mild anxiety symptoms (BAI = 10). Brivaracetam-related mood side effects were reported historically and led to discontinuation. Current emotional symptoms did not appear sufficient to account for the cognitive profile.

Summary and Impressions:  
This evaluation documents broadly Average intellectual functioning with relative weaknesses in working memory, confrontation naming, verbal list learning, delayed free recall, visual memory retrieval, and aspects of executive organization. Processing speed, semantic fluency, visuospatial reasoning, contextual verbal learning, and inhibitory control were comparatively preserved. Validity findings support interpretation of the obtained profile.

The memory pattern is mild and not cleanly lateralizing. Both verbal and visual retrieval were reduced, with stronger recognition and better performance for structured material. MRI reportedly shows hippocampal sclerosis, but the absence of specified MRI laterality and EEG localization precludes determining concordance between the cognitive findings and the suspected epileptogenic zone. Low Average confrontation naming raises concern for possible dominant temporal-system involvement but does not independently establish hemispheric language dominance.

From a surgical-risk perspective, resection involving the language-dominant mesial temporal region could carry risk for further decline in verbal learning, delayed recall, and naming. Nondominant temporal intervention could similarly affect visual or spatial memory, although the present visual-memory findings are not strongly lateralizing. Cognitive risk should therefore be integrated with seizure semiology, MRI review, scalp or intracranial EEG, and functional language/memory mapping. If resection is not feasible or localization remains uncertain, neuromodulation options such as the NeuroPace RNS System may be discussed by the epilepsy team.

Recommendations:  
1. Review findings at PRC in conjunction with MRI laterality, video-EEG data, and the full neurologic evaluation.  
2. Obtain task-based fMRI for language lateralization and assessment of temporal activation before any dominant temporal intervention.  
3. Consider Wada testing if fMRI is inconclusive, bilateral, or discordant with other localization data.  
4. Continue ASM management with the treating epileptologist; current medication is zonisamide. Brivaracetam should remain documented as previously discontinued due to mood side effects.  
5. Use external memory supports, including calendars, alarms, written routines, and spaced-retrieval strategies. Present new verbal information in organized, contextual form when possible.  
6. Repeat neuropsychological evaluation approximately 6-12 months s/p any surgical or neuromodulation intervention, or sooner if there is a significant cognitive change.
