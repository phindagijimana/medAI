Referral Question:  
Diagnostic/presurgical neuropsychological baseline to characterize cognitive functioning in the context of generalized epilepsy and MRI evidence of focal cortical dysplasia. Findings will inform multidisciplinary discussion at PRC regarding treatment options and cognitive risk.

Relevant History:  
The patient has a h/o generalized epilepsy with onset at age 9. Current seizures are described as myoclonic events occurring approximately once per month. Etiology has been classified as structural, with MRI demonstrating focal cortical dysplasia. Available records characterize the epilepsy as drug responsive. No EEG lateralization or localization data were available for review; therefore, relationships between the structural lesion, seizure network, and cognitive findings should be interpreted cautiously.

Current antiseizure medication is valproate; dose and schedule were not provided. Previously trialed perampanel, discontinued due to rash, and zonisamide, discontinued due to cognitive side effects. The patient denies febrile seizures in childhood and denies urinary incontinence associated with events. Educational, occupational, developmental, and psychiatric histories were not sufficiently documented to permit detailed conclusions regarding premorbid functioning. Family hx is notable for a brother with stroke.

Behavioral Observations:

Appearance and Cooperation:  
Arrived appropriately groomed. In NAD. Alert and oriented x3. Cooperative throughout the interview and testing.

Speech and Language:  
Speech fluent and normally articulated. No overt paraphasic errors. Comprehension adequate for multistep test instructions.

Mood and Affect:  
Mood appeared neutral. Affect appropriate and congruent. No marked behavioral evidence of depression, anxiety, or psychosis.

Effort and Engagement:  
Consistent task engagement. Frustration tolerance adequate. Test of Memory Malingering Trial 2 = 50/50, indicating adequate performance validity. Reliable Digit Span was also within acceptable limits. Results are considered a valid estimate of current cognitive functioning.

Tests Administered:  
WAIS-IV; WMS-IV Logical Memory and Visual Reproduction; California Verbal Learning Test–Third Edition (CVLT-3); Rey-Osterrieth Complex Figure (ROCF); Boston Naming Test (BNT); Controlled Oral Word Association Test; semantic fluency; Trail Making Test Parts A and B; Stroop Color-Word Test; Wisconsin Card Sorting Test (WCST); Grooved Pegboard; Beck Depression Inventory–II (BDI-II); Beck Anxiety Inventory (BAI); and performance-validity measures.

Results:

Intellectual Functioning:  
WAIS-IV Full Scale IQ was 91 (27th percentile; Average), with variability across domains. Verbal Comprehension Index was 98 (45th percentile; Average), Perceptual Reasoning Index 94 (34th percentile; Average), Working Memory Index 89 (23rd percentile; Low Average), and Processing Speed Index 81 (10th percentile; Low Average). General verbal and nonverbal reasoning abilities were broadly preserved, while timed output represented a relative weakness.

Attention/Processing Speed:  
Auditory attention and working memory ranged from Low Average to Average (Digit Span scaled score = 8; Arithmetic scaled score = 8). Trail Making Test Part A was Low Average (T=40), without errors. WAIS-IV Coding was Borderline (scaled score = 6), and Symbol Search was Low Average (scaled score = 7). The pattern indicates reduced processing efficiency rather than a primary attentional disorder. Medication effects, including possible cognitive slowing associated with valproate, cannot be excluded.

Language:  
Confrontation naming was Average on the BNT (T=48). Phonemic verbal fluency was Low Average (T=41), whereas semantic fluency for animals was Average (T=47). Vocabulary and verbal abstract reasoning were Average. There was no compelling evidence of focal language dysfunction or dominant temporal compromise on the current measures. Interpretation of language laterality remains limited by undocumented handedness and absent functional language-mapping data.

Verbal Memory:  
CVLT-3 acquisition across five learning trials was Low Average (Trials 1–5 standard score = 85), with a shallow but positive learning curve. Short-delay free recall was Low Average (scaled score = 7), and long-delay free recall was Borderline (scaled score = 6). Recognition discriminability improved to Average (scaled score = 9), suggesting inefficient retrieval with comparatively better retention when cueing was provided. WMS-IV Logical Memory immediate recall was Average (scaled score = 9), while delayed recall was Low Average (scaled score = 7); recognition was intact. Overall, verbal memory was mildly reduced, particularly for unstructured list learning and spontaneous delayed retrieval.

Visual Memory:  
ROCF copy was intact (32/36), reflecting adequate visuoconstruction and planning. Immediate recall was Low Average (T=42), and delayed recall was Low Average (T=40), with preserved recognition. WMS-IV Visual Reproduction immediate recall was Average (scaled score = 9), and delayed recall was Low Average (scaled score = 7). Visual memory findings were mildly reduced but not clearly lateralizing.

Executive Function:  
Trail Making Test Part B was Low Average (T=39), with one sequencing error that was self-corrected. Stroop inhibition was Low Average (T=41). On the WCST, the patient completed five categories, with total errors in the Average range (T=46) and mildly elevated perseverative responding (T=42). Findings suggest subtle inefficiency in cognitive flexibility and inhibition without a frank executive syndrome.

Visuospatial Functioning:  
WAIS-IV Block Design was Average (scaled score = 9). ROCF copy was organized and accurate. No evidence of visuospatial neglect, constructional apraxia, or a focal right-hemisphere visuospatial deficit.

Motor Functioning:  
Grooved Pegboard performance was Low Average bilaterally (dominant hand T=41; nondominant hand T=40), without meaningful lateralized discrepancy. No gross motor abnormality observed.

Mood/Personality:  
BDI-II score was 7, indicating minimal depressive symptoms. BAI score was 6, indicating minimal anxiety. Self-report did not suggest that emotional distress substantially affected test performance.

Summary and Impressions:  
Results demonstrate broadly Average intellectual functioning with relative weaknesses in processing speed, working memory, verbal list learning, delayed retrieval, and selected executive tasks. Confrontation naming, verbal reasoning, visuospatial construction, recognition memory, and basic problem solving were preserved. The memory pattern was characterized more by inefficient acquisition and retrieval than by rapid forgetting, and findings were not strongly lateralizing.

In the context of generalized myoclonic epilepsy, focal cortical dysplasia on MRI raises a potentially important electroclinical-imaging discrepancy. The present cognitive profile does not independently establish that the dysplasia represents the epileptogenic zone. Likewise, there is no clear neuropsychological signature of isolated dominant or nondominant temporal dysfunction. Mild diffuse inefficiency may reflect the broader epilepsy network, antiseizure medication effects, or both. Given drug-responsive status, risks and anticipated benefits of any surgical intervention require careful review at PRC.

Recommendations:  
1. Discuss findings at PRC with integration of seizure semiology, MRI, and video-EEG data.  
2. If focal intervention remains under consideration, obtain fMRI language mapping; consider Wada testing if fMRI is inconclusive or discordant.  
3. Consider additional prolonged video-EEG monitoring to clarify whether the focal cortical dysplasia participates in seizure onset.  
4. If resection, ablation, or neuromodulation is contemplated, counsel regarding possible decline in memory and processing efficiency. Options such as the NeuroPace RNS System should be considered only if supported by electroclinical localization and overall treatment goals.  
5. Repeat neuropsychological evaluation approximately 6–12 months s/p intervention, or sooner with meaningful cognitive change.  
6. Use external memory supports, structured routines, reduced multitasking, and additional time for speeded tasks.
