Referral Question:  
Diagnostic neuropsychological evaluation to characterize cognitive functioning in the setting of focal epilepsy and establish a baseline for PRC review. Assess for cognitive findings suggestive of temporal dysfunction and clarify potential cognitive risk should surgical treatment be considered in the future.

Relevant History:  
The patient has a h/o focal epilepsy, with focal aware seizures beginning at age 10. Epilepsy is associated with a pathogenic DEPDC5 c.4396C>T variant and is considered genetic in etiology. A paternal cousin reportedly had generalized epilepsy of childhood.

The patient is currently seizure-free, with zero seizures per month, on perampanel. Epilepsy is presently classified as drug-responsive. Levetiracetam was previously trialed and discontinued because of sedation; it is not a current medication. Referral materials also contained language describing “likely drug-resistant focal epilepsy.” This appears discordant with the current seizure-free, drug-responsive status and should be reconciled by the treating epilepsy team before surgical candidacy is determined.

Brain MRI reportedly demonstrated nonspecific white matter changes. Some referral wording characterized the imaging as possibly consistent with mesial temporal sclerosis; however, a definitive mesial temporal structural lesion was not established in the information available for this evaluation. No sufficiently detailed EEG localization or language-dominance data were available for interpretation. Educational, occupational, psychiatric, and substance-use histories were not fully documented in the referral record.

Behavioral Observations:

Appearance and Cooperation:  
Arrived appropriately groomed. In NAD. Alert and oriented. Cooperative throughout interview and testing.

Speech and Language:  
Speech fluent and normally articulated. No evident paraphasic errors. Comprehension adequate for multistep instructions.

Mood and Affect:  
Mood appeared euthymic. Affect appropriate and stable. No marked behavioral evidence of depression, anxiety, or psychosis.

Effort and Engagement:  
Task persistence and frustration tolerance adequate. Performance on a stand-alone symptom validity measure was within expectations (TOMM Trial 2 = 50/50). Embedded validity indices, including Reliable Digit Span, were also acceptable. Results are considered a valid estimate of current cognitive functioning.

Tests Administered:  
WAIS-IV; WMS-IV Logical Memory and Visual Reproduction; CVLT-3; Rey-Osterrieth Complex Figure Test (ROCF); Boston Naming Test; COWAT letter and animal fluency; Trail Making Test Parts A and B; Stroop Color-Word Test; Wisconsin Card Sorting Test; Grooved Pegboard; TOMM; BDI-II; and BAI.

Results:

Intellectual Functioning:  
WAIS-IV Full Scale IQ was Average (FSIQ = 98, 45th percentile). Verbal Comprehension was Average (VCI = 102, 55th percentile), as was Perceptual Reasoning (PRI = 100, 50th percentile). Working Memory fell in the Average range (WMI = 97, 42nd percentile). Processing Speed was Low Average (PSI = 89, 23rd percentile), representing a relative weakness rather than a global intellectual limitation.

Attention/Processing Speed:  
Auditory attention and working memory were Average (Digit Span scaled score = 10). Trail Making A was Low Average (T = 42), with no errors. Timed visual scanning and graphomotor output were mildly inefficient. Stroop word and color reading were Low Average to Average. Overall, basic attention was intact, with a relative reduction in processing speed.

Language:  
Confrontation naming was Average (BNT T = 48). Letter fluency was Low Average (T = 43), while semantic fluency was Average (T = 49). Verbal abstraction and vocabulary were intact. There was no clinically significant anomia or semantic-access deficit to suggest a clear dominant temporal language disturbance. Language dominance cannot be inferred from these behavioral findings alone.

Verbal Memory:  
On the CVLT-3, acquisition across five learning trials was Average (Trials 1–5 standard score = 96). Learning improved with repetition, without an abnormal intrusion pattern. Short-delay free recall was Average (scaled score = 9), and long-delay free recall was Low Average (scaled score = 8). Recognition discrimination was Average, suggesting effective retention of information that was successfully encoded.

WMS-IV Logical Memory immediate recall was Average (scaled score = 10), with Low Average delayed recall (scaled score = 8) and intact recognition. This profile indicates mild inefficiency in spontaneous retrieval of verbal information but not a rapid-forgetting pattern typical of a pronounced mesial temporal amnestic syndrome.

Visual Memory:  
ROCF copy was intact and well organized. Delayed recall was Average (T = 47). WMS-IV Visual Reproduction immediate and delayed recall were both Average (scaled scores = 10 and 9, respectively), with intact recognition. There was no material-specific visual memory impairment suggestive of clear right temporal dysfunction.

Executive Function:  
Trail Making B was Average (T = 46), with no set-loss errors. Stroop inhibition was Average (T = 48). On the WCST, conceptual problem-solving was intact, with six categories completed and perseverative responses in the Average range (T = 50). Executive functioning was broadly preserved.

Visuospatial Functioning:  
WAIS-IV Block Design was Average (scaled score = 10). ROCF construction was accurate, with adequate planning and spatial integration. No visuospatial deficit was identified.

Motor Functioning:  
Grooved Pegboard performance was Low Average bilaterally, without a meaningful lateralized discrepancy. Slower output appeared consistent with the broader processing-speed weakness rather than a focal motor deficit.

Mood/Personality:  
Self-report measures indicated minimal depressive symptoms (BDI-II = 6) and minimal anxiety symptoms (BAI = 5). Emotional factors did not appear to account for the observed cognitive profile.

Summary and Impressions:  
The evaluation was valid and revealed broadly Average intellectual functioning. Relative strengths included language comprehension, confrontation naming, visuospatial construction, visual memory, and executive functioning. Mild weaknesses were observed in processing speed and spontaneous delayed retrieval of verbal information. Recognition memory remained intact, and there was no evidence of rapid forgetting.

The findings are nonlateralizing. Specifically, there was no significant confrontation-naming deficit or verbal-memory storage impairment strongly implicating dominant mesial temporal dysfunction, and visual memory was intact. Thus, the cognitive profile does not independently support a focal temporal localization or confirm imaging findings consistent with mesial temporal sclerosis. Nonspecific white matter changes and possible medication effects may contribute to mild cognitive slowing.

Current data indicate drug-responsive focal epilepsy with seizure freedom on perampanel, notwithstanding referral language suggesting likely drug-resistant focal epilepsy. The discrepancy should be reviewed at PRC. At present, these results provide a useful baseline but do not, by themselves, support resective surgery or neuromodulation. If surgical treatment is later reconsidered, additional localization and language mapping would be necessary to r/o clinically meaningful postoperative cognitive risk.

Recommendations:  
1. Discuss findings at PRC and reconcile the conflicting characterization of drug-responsive versus likely drug-resistant focal epilepsy.  
2. Continue epilepsy management with the treating epileptologist; medication decisions remain deferred to that service.  
3. If invasive or resective treatment is reconsidered, obtain fMRI language mapping. Consider Wada testing if fMRI is inconclusive or discordant with EEG/imaging.  
4. Confirm MRI findings through expert neuroradiology review, specifically addressing whether there is objective evidence of mesial temporal sclerosis beyond nonspecific white matter changes.  
5. If seizures become drug-resistant, PRC may consider resection, ablation, or neuromodulation options such as the NeuroPace RNS System after appropriate localization.  
6. Repeat neuropsychological evaluation following any major treatment change or approximately 6–12 months s/p epilepsy surgery, if performed.
