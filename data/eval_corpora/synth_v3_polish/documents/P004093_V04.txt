Referral Question:  
Presurgical neuropsychological baseline in the context of drug-resistant focal epilepsy with focal-to-bilateral tonic-clonic seizures. Assess cognitive strengths and weaknesses, evaluate evidence for hemispheric or temporal dysfunction, and inform discussion at PRC.

Relevant History:  
The patient has a hx of genetically mediated focal epilepsy, with seizure onset at age 55. Typical events have been classified as focal-to-bilateral tonic-clonic seizures. The causative genetic finding is reported as an STXBP1 c.902A>G variant. Although the epilepsy has been characterized as drug resistant based on prior treatment history, the patient currently reports no seizures, corresponding to 0 events per month.

Current ASMs are carbamazepine and lacosamide; doses were not available for review. Previously trialed oxcarbazepine was discontinued due to rash. Levetiracetam and zonisamide were each discontinued due to GI intolerance. These prior agents are not current medications. The patient separately denies syncope and denies episodes of unexplained loss of consciousness apart from the established seizure disorder.

Brain MRI reportedly demonstrated nonspecific white matter changes without a described focal structural lesion. EEG findings and a proposed surgical target were not available in the reviewed information. Thus, the present cognitive findings should not be used independently to establish seizure onset laterality.

Educational, occupational, developmental, and psychiatric histories were not sufficiently documented in the available record. No additional assumptions regarding these variables were made in interpretation. Current ASMs, fatigue, and nonspecific white matter disease remain potential contributors to cognitive efficiency.

Results:

Intellectual Functioning:  
WAIS-IV overall ability fell within the Average range (FSIQ = 96, 39th percentile). Verbal Comprehension was Average (VCI = 100, 50th percentile), as was Perceptual Reasoning (PRI = 98, 45th percentile). Working Memory was Low Average (WMI = 89, 23rd percentile), while Processing Speed was also Low Average (PSI = 86, 18th percentile). The profile suggests generally intact reasoning abilities with relative inefficiency on timed and attentionally demanding tasks.

Attention/Processing Speed:  
Auditory attention and working memory ranged from Low Average to Average (Digit Span scaled score = 8; Arithmetic scaled score = 9). Trail Making A was Low Average (T = 41), without errors. WAIS-IV Coding was Low Average (scaled score = 7), and Symbol Search was Average (scaled score = 9). Overall, findings indicate mild slowing rather than a primary attentional disorder.

Language:  
Confrontation naming was Average on the Boston Naming Test (T = 49), with benefit from phonemic cues. Letter fluency was Low Average (T = 42), whereas semantic fluency was Average (T = 47). Verbal abstraction was Average (Similarities scaled score = 10). There was no convincing evidence of a dominant temporal language deficit. Because handedness, language dominance, EEG localization, and surgical target were not established, these findings do not substitute for formal language mapping.

Verbal Memory:  
On the CVLT-3, acquisition across five learning trials was Low Average (Trials 1–5 standard score = 87). Initial encoding was mildly reduced, though learning improved across repetition. Short-delay free recall was Low Average (scaled score = 7), and long-delay free recall was Low Average (scaled score = 7). Recognition discriminability was Average (scaled score = 9), suggesting inefficient retrieval with comparatively preserved retention and recognition.

WMS-IV Logical Memory immediate recall was Average (scaled score = 9), with Low Average delayed recall (scaled score = 7) and intact recognition. This pattern does not show rapid forgetting or a clear amnestic syndrome. Verbal memory is nevertheless a relative weakness and warrants consideration if dominant temporal surgery is contemplated.

Visual Memory:  
WMS-IV Visual Reproduction immediate recall was Average (scaled score = 10), and delayed recall was Average (scaled score = 9). Recognition was within normal limits. ROCF delayed recall was Low Average (T = 42), occurring in the context of a mildly disorganized initial approach. Visual retention was broadly preserved, without a compelling pattern of selective right temporal dysfunction.

Executive Function:  
Trail Making B was Low Average (T = 40), with no set-loss errors. Stroop inhibition was Average (T = 46). On the WCST, the patient completed five categories; perseverative responses were Average (T = 48). Executive abilities were broadly intact, although cognitive efficiency declined under time pressure and during tasks requiring rapid switching.

Visuospatial Functioning:  
Block Design was Average (scaled score = 10). ROCF copy was within normal limits, though construction was somewhat piecemeal. No visuospatial neglect, gross constructional deficit, or clear lateralized visual-perceptual abnormality was evident.

Motor Functioning:  
Grooved Pegboard performance was Low Average bilaterally, without a clinically meaningful asymmetry. Fine-motor speed was mildly reduced but not clearly lateralizing.

Mood/Personality:  
BDI-II and BAI responses fell within the minimal range. Self-report did not indicate clinically significant depressive or anxiety symptoms at the time of evaluation. Mood findings should be monitored longitudinally given the psychosocial burden of chronic epilepsy and potential surgical planning.

Summary and Impressions:  
This valid evaluation documents broadly Average intellectual and reasoning abilities with relative weaknesses in processing speed, working memory, verbal list learning, and delayed verbal retrieval. Confrontation naming, semantic fluency, visuospatial skills, visual memory, and most executive functions were preserved. The memory pattern reflects inefficient acquisition and retrieval rather than rapid forgetting, with improvement on recognition testing.

The profile is mildly nonspecific and does not provide strong evidence for isolated dominant or nondominant temporal dysfunction. Mild cognitive inefficiency could reflect the combined effects of epilepsy, carbamazepine and lacosamide treatment, fatigue, and MRI-documented nonspecific white matter changes. Correlation with video-EEG, seizure semiology, imaging, and functional language studies is essential.

If dominant temporal resection is considered, there may be a moderate risk for further decline in verbal learning and memory because these abilities are already relatively weak but remain functionally intact. If nondominant temporal intervention is proposed, current visual memory is preserved, creating some potential for postoperative decline. Cognitive data alone do not establish laterality or identify a resection target.

Recommendations:  
1. Discuss findings at multidisciplinary PRC with MRI, video-EEG, genetic, and semiologic data.  
2. Obtain fMRI language mapping before any dominant-hemisphere resection. Consider Wada testing if fMRI is inconclusive or discordant with other localization data.  
3. Continue current ASM management through Epileptology. Monitor processing speed and memory for medication-related effects.  
4. If resection is not feasible and seizures recur despite adequate medication trials, consider neuromodulation options as clinically appropriate, including the NeuroPace RNS System when a suitable network can be defined.  
5. Use written schedules, repetition, cueing, and reduced-distraction settings for complex information.  
6. Repeat neuropsychological evaluation approximately 6–12 months s/p surgical or neuromodulation intervention, or sooner if there is meaningful cognitive decline.
