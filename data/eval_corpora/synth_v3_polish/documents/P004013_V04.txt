Referral Question:  
Comprehensive neuropsychological baseline to characterize cognitive functioning in the context of epilepsy and focal cortical dysplasia, and to inform multidisciplinary discussion at PRC regarding potential treatment planning.

Relevant History:  
The patient has a hx of combined generalized and focal epilepsy, with seizure onset at age 10. Reported events have included focal impaired-awareness seizures. The patient denies preceding aura and denies complete loss of consciousness. Current seizure frequency is 0/month, with the patient reporting ongoing seizure freedom on the present regimen. The epilepsy is considered drug responsive.

Current ASMs include topiramate and valproate. Lamotrigine was previously trialed and discontinued because of inadequate seizure control. Available MRI information identifies focal cortical dysplasia, supporting a structural etiology. Lesion laterality and detailed EEG localization were not provided for the present evaluation; consequently, concordance among MRI, EEG, and cognitive findings cannot be fully assessed.

Detailed psychiatric, developmental, educational, and occupational records were not available. The patient did not endorse clinically significant current depression or anxiety on self-report measures. No conclusion regarding a h/o specific learning disorder or attention-deficit disorder can be made from the available background information alone.

Behavioral Observations:

Appearance and Cooperation:  
Arrived appropriately dressed and groomed. In NAD. Alert and oriented x3. Cooperative throughout the evaluation.

Speech and Language:  
Speech fluent and normally articulated. No paraphasic errors or gross word-finding difficulty during conversation. Comprehension adequate for multistep instructions.

Mood and Affect:  
Mood described as generally stable. Affect appropriate and congruent. No behavioral evidence of severe anxiety, depression, or psychosis.

Effort and Engagement:  
Consistent task engagement. Frustration tolerance adequate. Test-taking behavior appropriate. TOMM Trial 2 = 50/50, indicating adequate performance validity. Embedded validity indices, including Reliable Digit Span, were also within acceptable limits. Results are considered a valid estimate of current cognitive functioning.

Tests Administered:  
WAIS-IV; WMS-IV Logical Memory and Visual Reproduction; CVLT-3; Rey-Osterrieth Complex Figure Test (ROCF); Trail Making Test Parts A and B; Boston Naming Test (BNT); Controlled Oral Word Association Test; animal fluency; Wisconsin Card Sorting Test (WCST); Stroop Color-Word Test; Grooved Pegboard; Beck Depression Inventory-II (BDI-II); Beck Anxiety Inventory (BAI); Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9); Test of Memory Malingering (TOMM).

Results:

Intellectual Functioning:  
WAIS-IV Full Scale IQ was 94 (34th percentile; Average). Verbal Comprehension Index was 98 (45th percentile; Average), Perceptual Reasoning Index was 100 (50th percentile; Average), Working Memory Index was 92 (30th percentile; Average), and Processing Speed Index was 84 (14th percentile; Low Average). The profile reflects broadly preserved reasoning abilities with a relative weakness in rapid visual processing.

Attention/Processing Speed:  
Auditory attention and working memory were Low Average to Average (Digit Span scaled score = 8; Arithmetic scaled score = 9). Trail Making Test Part A was Low Average (T = 42). Timed visual scanning and symbol transcription were comparatively weak, consistent with the reduced Processing Speed Index. No pervasive deficit in basic attention was identified.

Language:  
Confrontation naming was Average (BNT T = 47). Phonemic verbal fluency was Low Average (FAS T = 43), while semantic fluency was Average (animals T = 46). Verbal abstraction and vocabulary were Average. These findings do not demonstrate a prominent aphasic syndrome or a clear dominant-temporal naming deficit.

Verbal Memory:  
On the CVLT-3, total learning across five trials was Low Average (T = 43), with a positive but somewhat shallow learning curve. Short-delay free recall was Low Average (z = -0.5), and long-delay free recall fell at the lower end of the Low Average range (z = -1.0). Recognition discriminability was Low Average (z = -0.5), without excessive false-positive errors.

WMS-IV Logical Memory immediate recall was Low Average (scaled score = 8), with delayed recall in the Borderline-to-Low Average range (scaled score = 7). Recognition was within the 17th–25th percentile range. The pattern suggests mild inefficiency in verbal acquisition and retrieval rather than rapid forgetting or a dense amnestic syndrome.

Visual Memory:  
WMS-IV Visual Reproduction immediate recall was Average (scaled score = 10), and delayed recall was Average (scaled score = 9). Recognition fell within the 26th–50th percentile range. ROCF delayed recall was Average (T = 48). Visual retention was intact and modestly stronger than verbal memory, although the discrepancy was not sufficiently marked to establish reliable hemispheric lateralization.

Executive Function:  
Trail Making Test Part B was Low Average (T = 38), reflecting slowed set shifting. On the WCST, the patient completed four categories, with total errors in the Low Average range (T = 42). Stroop inhibition was Low Average (T = 39). Verbal abstraction remained Average. Overall, there was mild inefficiency on speeded executive tasks without evidence of severe perseveration or loss of conceptual reasoning.

Visuospatial:  
ROCF copy was intact (32/36), with an organized approach and no neglect. WAIS-IV Block Design was Average (scaled score = 10). No clinically significant visuoconstructional impairment was observed.

Motor:  
Grooved Pegboard performance was Low Average with the dominant hand (T = 43) and Average with the nondominant hand (T = 45). No meaningful lateralized motor slowing was evident.

Mood/Personality:  
BDI-II score was 8, BAI score was 5, and PHQ-9 score was 4, all within minimal symptom ranges. Current self-report does not suggest a major depressive or anxiety syndrome. Continued monitoring remains appropriate given the psychosocial burden associated with chronic epilepsy.

Summary and Impressions:  
The evaluation produced valid findings. General intellectual functioning was Average. Relative strengths included verbal reasoning, perceptual reasoning, confrontation naming, visuospatial construction, and visual memory. Mild weaknesses were observed in processing speed, speeded executive functioning, and verbal learning/retrieval. There was no evidence of global cognitive decline, severe memory impairment, or a focal aphasic syndrome.

The cognitive profile is mildly variable but does not provide strong lateralizing or localizing evidence. Visual memory was somewhat stronger than verbal memory, but language and naming remained intact. Because the laterality of the focal cortical dysplasia and detailed EEG findings were unavailable, the neuropsychological data cannot be judged fully concordant or discordant with the structural lesion. Topiramate and valproate may contribute to cognitive slowing in some patients, although medication effects cannot be isolated from epilepsy-related and individual factors in this evaluation.

The patient is currently seizure free at 0 seizures/month and is characterized as drug responsive. Thus, the present findings are most useful as a cognitive baseline. If PRC is considering an intervention despite current seizure control, surgical risk cannot be estimated adequately without lesion laterality, language-dominance data, and electroclinical localization. Neuropsychological results alone do not establish candidacy for resection or a NeuroPace RNS System.

Recommendations:  
1. Review these findings at PRC together with MRI, video-EEG, seizure semiology, and treatment history.  
2. Confirm lesion laterality and electroclinical localization before drawing surgical conclusions.  
3. Obtain fMRI language mapping if dominant-hemisphere surgery is under consideration. Consider Wada testing to r/o atypical language or memory organization if fMRI is inconclusive or discordant.  
4. Continue follow-up with Epileptology regarding topiramate and valproate. Any medication changes should be directed by the treating neurologist given current seizure freedom.  
5. Use compensatory strategies for slowed processing and verbal retrieval, including written instructions, repetition, external reminders, and additional time for complex tasks.  
6. Repeat neuropsychological evaluation following any major treatment intervention, typically at 6–12 months, or earlier if clinically significant cognitive change occurs.
