Referral Question:  
Presurgical/diagnostic neuropsychological baseline in the context of focal epilepsy. Assessment of cognitive strengths and weaknesses, including memory and language functioning, to inform discussion at PRC and potential future treatment planning.

Relevant History:  
The patient has a hx of focal epilepsy with onset at age 11. Current events are characterized as focal to bilateral tonic-clonic seizures, occurring approximately four times per month. Etiology has been classified as structural, although the available brain MRI was interpreted as normal. No EEG localization or lateralization findings were provided for review. The patient denies a h/o CNS infection.

Current antiseizure medication is carbamazepine; dose and schedule were not available. Epilepsy is characterized in the available record as drug-responsive. No prior ASM trials were documented. Psychiatric, developmental, educational, occupational, and family histories were not sufficiently detailed in the referral materials; consequently, their potential contributions to the current profile cannot be fully assessed. Handedness and language dominance are also unknown. No allergies were documented.

Mood and Affect:  
Mood neutral. Affect appropriate and stable. No behavioral evidence of marked anxiety, depression, psychosis, or disinhibition during the evaluation.

Effort and Engagement:  
Engagement was consistent across the examination. Test of Memory Malingering Trial 2 = 50/50, indicating adequate performance validity. Embedded validity indicators were also within acceptable limits. Results are considered a valid estimate of current cognitive functioning.

Tests Administered:  
WAIS-IV; Trail Making Test Parts A and B; Boston Naming Test; COWAT letter and animal fluency; CVLT-3; WMS-IV Logical Memory and Visual Reproduction; Rey-Osterrieth Complex Figure; Wisconsin Card Sorting Test; Stroop Color-Word Test; Grooved Pegboard; BDI-II; BAI; Test of Memory Malingering.

Results:

Intellectual Functioning:  
WAIS-IV Full Scale IQ was 95 (37th percentile; Average). Verbal Comprehension Index = 93 (32nd percentile; Average), Perceptual Reasoning Index = 100 (50th percentile; Average), Working Memory Index = 92 (30th percentile; Average), and Processing Speed Index = 89 (23rd percentile; Low Average). The profile was broadly consistent, with a relative weakness in speeded visual output.

Attention/Processing Speed:  
Auditory attention and working memory were Low Average to Average (Digit Span scaled score = 8; Arithmetic scaled score = 9). Trail Making Test Part A was Low Average (T = 41), without errors. WAIS-IV Coding was Low Average (scaled score = 7), while Symbol Search was Average (scaled score = 9). Findings indicate mildly reduced processing efficiency without evidence of a primary attentional disorder.

Language:  
Confrontation naming was Low Average (Boston Naming Test T = 43), with benefit from phonemic cueing. Letter fluency was Average (T = 48), and semantic fluency was Low Average (T = 42). Vocabulary and verbal abstract reasoning were Average. The mild weakness in confrontation naming may reflect reduced lexical retrieval efficiency, but it is not sufficiently severe or specific to establish dominant temporal dysfunction. Language dominance cannot be inferred in the absence of handedness data and functional mapping.

Verbal Memory:  
On the CVLT-3, overall acquisition across five learning trials was Low Average (Trials 1-5 standard score = 84). Initial acquisition was Borderline, with improvement across repetitions. Short-delay free recall was Low Average (z = -1.0), and long-delay free recall was Low Average (z = -1.0). Recognition discriminability was Average (z = -0.5), with limited false-positive errors. WMS-IV Logical Memory immediate recall was Low Average (scaled score = 7), while delayed recall was Average (scaled score = 8). Recognition was intact.

This pattern reflects inefficient initial encoding and retrieval rather than rapid forgetting. Retention of successfully learned verbal information was broadly preserved, which is not a classic amnestic pattern.

Visual Memory:  
WMS-IV Visual Reproduction immediate recall was Average (scaled score = 10), with Average delayed recall (scaled score = 9). ROCF delayed recall was Average (T = 50), and recognition was intact. Visual memory represented a relative strength compared with verbal list learning. There was no clear evidence of right mesial temporal memory dysfunction.

Executive Function:  
Trail Making Test Part B was Average (T = 46), with no set-loss errors. WCST performance was broadly Average, including categories completed and perseverative responses (T = 49). Stroop inhibition was Low Average (T = 42). Verbal abstraction was Average. Overall executive functioning was preserved, with mild inefficiency under speeded inhibitory demands.

Visuospatial Functioning:  
WAIS-IV Block Design was Average (scaled score = 10). ROCF copy was intact and well organized. No visuospatial distortion, neglect, or constructional apraxia was observed.

Motor Functioning:  
Grooved Pegboard performance was Low Average bilaterally (dominant hand T = 41; nondominant hand T = 40), without a meaningful lateralizing discrepancy. Interpretation is limited because handedness was not documented.

Mood/Personality:  
BDI-II and BAI scores fell within the minimal range. Self-report did not indicate clinically significant depressive or anxiety symptoms at the time of evaluation.

Summary and Impressions:  
The evaluation documents broadly Average intellectual functioning with relative strengths in visuospatial reasoning, visual memory, and retention of learned material. Mild weaknesses were observed in processing speed, confrontation naming, semantic retrieval, and initial verbal learning. Verbal information was retained adequately once encoded, arguing against a pronounced mesial temporal amnestic syndrome. Performance validity was adequate, and the findings are considered interpretable.

The current pattern is mildly asymmetric at the cognitive level, with verbal learning and naming somewhat weaker than visual memory; however, it does not provide definitive localization or lateralization. Interpretation is constrained by the normal MRI, unavailable EEG localization data, undocumented handedness, and unknown language dominance. If a dominant temporal intervention is contemplated, there may be some risk of decline in naming and verbal memory, particularly given the existing Low Average verbal learning profile. The magnitude of risk cannot be estimated reliably without a defined surgical target and language lateralization data.

Recommendations:  
1. Review findings at PRC together with seizure semiology, video-EEG, MRI, and other available localization data.  
2. If resective or ablative treatment involving the temporal region is considered, obtain fMRI language mapping. Consider Wada testing if fMRI is inconclusive or discordant with other data.  
3. If MRI shows progression, obtain PET/SPECT for concordance with electroclinical findings.  
4. Given the designation of drug-responsive epilepsy, clarify current seizure burden, medication adherence, carbamazepine dose, and treatment goals before advancing to invasive evaluation.  
5. If seizures become medically refractory and resection is not feasible, neuromodulation options, including the NeuroPace RNS System, may be discussed at PRC as clinically appropriate.  
6. Repeat neuropsychological evaluation s/p intervention, typically at 6-12 months, or earlier if there is an unexpected cognitive change.  
7. Use repetition, written reminders, and structured encoding strategies for verbally presented information.
